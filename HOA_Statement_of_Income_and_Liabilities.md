## Background

At the request of Scarborough Glen owners, this report has been prepared for the **Master Homeowners Association (HOA)**. It shows the money that came in and went out from **January 1 through June 30, 2026**. It also looks at the big repair costs the HOA can expect in the future, based on two engineering studies.

Unlike the four condo associations, the HOA does **not own any homes or residential buildings**. It takes care of the shared property for all **136 units**:

| Area | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Grounds | Lawns, landscaping and trees, including the land around all four condos |
| Paving | Private roads, driveways, walks and parking areas |
| Recreation | Clubhouse, pool and fitness room |
| Site features | Fences, street lights and signs |
| Shared utilities | Sewer, water and drainage pipes outside the buildings or under their concrete slabs |
| Insurance | The condos' insurance, with the cost shared equally by all 136 owners |

Most of this list comes from the **First Amendment** to the HOA's Declaration and By-Laws (§1(d) and §22.1). The HOA pays for these services with the dues it collects from the four condo associations. Those dues are its main source of income.

This report is meant to help owners understand the HOA's money today, its savings (reserves), and the big repair bills ahead. It was prepared by the HOA Treasurer. It is **unaudited**: it has not been audited by an outside accounting firm, it is not intended to represent audited financial statements in accordance with U.S. GAAP (the standard accounting rules), and no assurance is expressed on the projections.

The Becht study looks at the common property of Scarborough Glen as a whole. The Falcon report splits costs by entity. The repair figures below are the part that belongs to the HOA, using the per-entity quantities from the Falcon report and the HOA's responsibilities under the First Amendment.

This statement is meant to be issued twice a year, like Condo IV's: a mid-year edition (January–June), issued in the fall, and a full-year edition (January–December), issued in the spring. This is the first, mid-year 2026 edition. The next edition will cover all of 2026.

### Why the HOA has no adopted plan

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| March 2022 | The Falcon Group delivered an engineering study of the reserves for the HOA and all four condos. |
| Since 2022 | The HOA board has **not defined a maintenance plan, voted on one, or acted on the engineering reports** or any other long-term planning. |
| February 3, 2026 | Becht Engineering delivered a second study, ordered through the management company. It is **still marked "DRAFT,"** and the board has not voted on it. |
| What Becht found | Reserves for the whole community, condos included, are only **14.57% funded**. The HOA put **$38,238** into reserves in the prior year. Becht's lowest recommendation is **$219,984 a year**. |

Without a plan, the HOA keeps all reserve money in one pool and spends it by case-by-case board votes (see *Reserve labels on the books*). In the Treasurer's view, the management company, Heritage Management, has given the board **little help** in moving this work forward.

Without a written plan saying who maintains what, each dispute over who pays, such as sprinklers or sewer lines, gets settled one case at a time. That leads to refunds, arguments and lawsuits, such as the one described in *Pending Legal Action* at the end of this report. Lenders and insurers also look at whether the HOA has a reserve plan and follows it.

**This report is the first HOA document to tell owners the HOA's income and liabilities.** Condo IV has already sent its owners a report like this one. The hope is that sharing these numbers openly will push the HOA board to finally vote on a maintenance plan and on the same three reserve funds Condo IV uses:

| Fund | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Long-Term Maintenance** | Saving for big planned repairs, such as repaving roads |
| **Operating Reserve** | A cushion of a few months of everyday costs |
| **Emergency Reserve** | Money for sudden repairs that insurance will not cover |

This plan pays for each repair when it comes due. It does **not** need a huge amount in the bank today. The savings balance goes up and down as bills are paid.

> **How the Emergency Reserve target was set.** The HOA's $200,000 target is a placeholder based on its largest shared assets, the clubhouse and pool. Condo IV sized its Emergency Reserve differently: only for what insurance will not pay in a bad year ($125,000). Under the First Amendment, the HOA insures the condo buildings (§22.1) and carries out rebuilding after a fire or other casualty (§22.2), and Condo IV's target assumes the HOA pays the policy deductible. The HOA also owns the sewer, water and drainage pipes outside the buildings and under their slabs (§1(d)(iii)). Insurance usually does not cover those pipes when they wear out or fail; the January 2026 sewer refunds ($16,355.94) are an example. Once the master policy's deductible and coverage are confirmed, the HOA's target should be re-sized the same way: the deductibles the HOA would pay, plus one uninsured pipe or retaining-wall failure, plus a cushion for its own clubhouse and pool claims.

## Pending Legal Action
*Maria Fernandez v. Scarborough Glen Homeowners Association, Inc., et al. — New York State Supreme Court, Westchester County, Index No. 62111/2026*

### What happened, according to the lawsuit

The HOA board has seven seats: one each for Condos I, II and III, three for Condo IV, and the At-Large seat. Four votes make a majority.

At a Zoom board meeting on **December 15, 2025**, a motion was made to remove Ms. Fernandez as President "due to lack of confidence." The vote was **4 to 3**. Wong, Anderson, Venturini and Sunberg voted yes. Fernandez, Lampone and Conners voted no. Right after that, the board chose Mr. Venturini as President and Ms. Sunberg as Vice President.

At the same meeting, the board voted 4 to 3 to settle a claim Condo IV had made against the HOA over **lawn sprinkler costs**. Condo IV had asked the HOA to pay about **$66,999.67** for sprinkler upkeep. The HOA board had said no twice, in January and February 2025. The settlement had the HOA pay Condo IV half of seven years of sprinkler costs, **$30,249.06**, and take over the sprinklers going forward.

### What the HOA's own rules say about sprinklers

The lawsuit does not mention it, but the HOA's governing documents speak to this question. The **First Amendment to the HOA Declaration and By-Laws** was recorded February 14, 2001 and approved by at least 80% of owners. It lists the **"Common Areas"** the HOA must take care of (§1(d)):

| Item | What it covers |
|---|---|
| (i) | The **land** of all four condos |
| (ii) | All **installations outside the units**, on condo land, that serve the community, "such as heat, power, light, gas, telephone, television and **water**" |
| (iii) | All **sewer, water and drainage pipes** outside the buildings or under their concrete slabs |
| (iv) | All **parking areas** |
| (v) | All **walks and driveways** |
| (vi) | All **private roads** |

Section 13 says the HOA must "operate, manage, repair and maintain the Common Areas." Section 19.3 says the HOA board must do "all maintenance work, repairs and replacements" for them. Section 20.3 says these costs are shared **equally by all 136 owners**.

The condos keep their **buildings**: foundations, outside walls, and "any terraces, decks, porches and exterior stairs" (§13). So the rule is **not** simply "everything outside the walls belongs to the HOA." Decks, porches and outside stairs stay with each condo.

The amendment **does not use the word "sprinkler."** But lawn sprinklers are water lines and fixtures, outside the buildings, on condo land. Read plainly, that puts them under items (i), (ii) and (iii), which would make sprinklers an HOA job **for all four condos**. The same section is why this report already treats driveways and walks on condo land as HOA costs (see *Future Major Repairs and Replacements*).

**This has happened before.** The HOA's books show the same pattern over and over. A condo or an owner paid for something the amendment makes an HOA job, such as pipes, water or sprinklers outside the buildings. Later the HOA paid them back:

| When | What the HOA paid back | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| 2023–2024 | Owners who paid for water used on the common grounds | about $1,838 |
| Sep 2023 | Condo I, for landscaping it paid from its own account | $948.28 |
| Aug 2025 | 34 refunds to Condo IV owners for sprinkler repairs | $7,166.07 |
| Oct 2025 | 10 more owner refunds for 2022 sprinkler repairs | $2,457.86 |
| Dec 2025 | Condo IV, for seven years of sprinkler costs (the settlement) | $30,249.06 |
| Jan 2026 | Condo III, for sewer line repairs at units 603 and 803 | $11,200.00 |
| Jan 2026 | Condo IV, for a sewer line repair at unit 1003 | $5,155.94 |
| **Total** | | **about $59,015** |

The August and October 2025 sprinkler refunds were paid while Ms. Fernandez was HOA President. The sewer refunds fall under item (iii) of the amendment, pipes "outside of the buildings… or under the concrete slabs." The HOA's general ledgers only go back to mid-2023, and this pattern shows up as far back as those records go. It likely started earlier, but there are no ledgers to check. Each time a cost lands in the wrong place, it takes extra work and extra money to fix later. That is a good reason for the board to write down, once and for all, who pays for what, using the amendment as the guide.

Not everyone has read it that way. The HOA board voted against Condo IV's claim twice in early 2025, and the lawsuit quotes a former HOA lawyer who called the HOA's case "a slam dunk." The HOA's lawyers had not finished a written opinion when the board voted to settle. **The court will not necessarily decide who owns the sprinklers.** The lawsuit is mainly about *how* the vote was taken and *who* was allowed to vote.

### Why this matters to owners

| Issue | What it could mean for owners |
|---|---|
| **HOA money is already involved** | The **$30,249.06** sprinkler settlement was paid to Condo IV on **December 18, 2025**, out of the HOA's **reserve savings account** (the Webster Bank capital reserve account). The HOA board has **not yet voted on who is responsible for which repairs**. Other than a "Road Reserve" label, the HOA has **never set up separate reserve funds** for specific projects; all reserve money sits in **one pool** in one bank account (see *Reserve labels on the books*). Nothing was ever set aside for sprinklers. So the settlement did not take money away from any particular project. It came out of HOA savings that all four condos pay into. The settlement also said the HOA would take over the sprinklers going forward, but the HOA has **not yet adopted a maintenance plan** for them. If the court cancels the settlement, the board would have to decide whether to ask Condo IV for the money back. It would then face the same question for the **other condos the HOA has paid back under the same amendment**, such as the January 2026 sewer-line refunds to Condo III ($11,200) and Condo IV ($5,155.94) (see *This has happened before*, above). Asking only Condo IV to repay would be hard to justify, since all of these payments rest on the same reading of the First Amendment. |
| **Past board decisions could be undone** | If the court agrees with Ms. Fernandez, decisions made by the board since December 15, 2025 could be cancelled. That could include officer elections, the sprinkler settlement, votes taken by email, and possibly the January 2026 sewer-line refunds to Condo III and Condo IV, if the same four-vote majority approved them. |
| **Legal costs** | The HOA is a respondent, so it will have to answer the lawsuit. Lawyers' fees would come out of HOA funds, which all four condos pay into through their dues. The HOA's 2026 legal budget is **$10,000**. The HOA carries directors-and-officers (D&O) insurance, which is meant to protect board members sued for board decisions. **Whether that policy covers this case is not yet known.** If it does not, the HOA pays the lawyers itself. That includes more than its own lawyer: the HOA By-Laws (Article XIX) say the HOA **must cover the legal fees of directors and officers** sued because of their board roles, unless they are found guilty of gross negligence or willful misconduct. Four board members are named in this case. Even if the policy does cover it, the HOA would usually still pay a deductible first. Costs above the $10,000 budget would have to come from somewhere else. The checking account had only about $12,200 at June 30, so the money would likely come from the **reserve savings account** or from a **special assessment** (an extra charge to owners). |
| **Harder for buyers to get a mortgage** | When someone applies for a loan to buy a unit here, the lender sends a questionnaire that asks whether the HOA or condo is involved in a lawsuit. Under Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac rules, which most standard mortgages follow, a pending lawsuit against the HOA can make the community **ineligible** for those loans unless the lender decides the case falls under a narrow exception. FHA loans have a similar review. Buyers with **moderate incomes** depend most on these standard loans, with low down payments and normal interest rates. If those loans are not available, buyers may need a larger down payment or a higher-rate loan, or they may not be able to buy at all. That can mean fewer buyers and lower sale prices for **every owner** who wants to sell. |
| **Must be reported in audited statements** | This report is not audited. But when the HOA's financial statements are audited under GAAP (the standard accounting rules), the auditor must ask the HOA's lawyer about pending lawsuits. The rules then require the case to be **described in the notes** to the audited statements if a loss is reasonably possible, and **recorded as a liability** if a loss becomes likely and the amount can be estimated. Neither is known yet. That is why it appears above as a **contingent** liability with no dollar amount. Lenders, insurers and buyers read those audited statements. |
| **Higher insurance costs** | Insurance companies ask about pending lawsuits when the HOA applies for or renews its policies. A claim on the directors-and-officers (D&O) policy, or a lawsuit the insurer learns about at renewal, can lead to **higher premiums**, a larger deductible, new exclusions, or fewer companies willing to quote. Those costs are paid from HOA dues, by all 136 owners. |
